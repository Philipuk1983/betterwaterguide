The Clearly Filtered Water Pitcher is a strong no-install option for buyers who want to look beyond chlorine taste and odor, but only after one important distinction: the current pitcher is NSF certified to Standards 42 and 372, while its much broader contaminant-reduction table comes from additional third-party laboratory testing. Those are both useful forms of evidence; they are not interchangeable.

Buy it when a municipal water report or appropriate test gives you a specific treatment question, the published performance table addresses it, and a gravity pitcher fits the household’s refill routine. Skip it when the source is microbiologically unsafe or unknown, the entire home needs treatment, or the household will resent filling a half-gallon reservoir several times a day.

## Quick verdict

**Best fit:** a renter, small household, or one-tap drinking-water user who wants no plumbing work and will track filter use.

**Main strength:** unusually extensive published performance data for a gravity pitcher, with a current 100-gallon rated capacity and explicit operating limits.

**Main compromise:** certification is narrower than the full third-party test table, and high daily use can turn the nominal six-month interval into a much shorter replacement cycle.

**Do not use it for:** microbiologically unsafe or unknown water, emergency disinfection, whole-house scale, or a contaminant that the current performance sheet does not substantiate under relevant conditions.

## The evidence ladder buyers should use

Pitcher marketing often compresses “certified,” “tested,” and “removes” into one impression. The safer buying method is to separate the evidence into layers.

| Evidence layer | What the current documentation establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| NSF/ANSI 42 certification | Certified chlorine taste-and-odor reduction claim | Certification for every health-related reduction on the broader table |
| NSF/ANSI 372 certification | Product meets the standard’s lead-content requirements for materials | Lead reduction from the water |
| Additional third-party performance testing | Controlled laboratory results for the named influent, conditions, capacity, and reductions shown | The same result in every home or certification to every cited protocol |
| Your utility report or lab result | Which named concerns are present in your water | Whether a pitcher’s flow and ownership routine fit your household |
| In-home follow-up | Whether treatment still matches the problem and maintenance plan | A substitute for certified testing when health or compliance is at stake |

Clearly Filtered’s November 2025 performance page identifies the system as a gravity-fed pitcher, gives a 100-gallon rated capacity, names replacement element CF-PRF, and states an operating range of 38–85°F. It also says the pitcher and replacement filter are certified to NSF/ANSI 42 and 372. The page separately explains that additional results came from an independent ISO 17025-accredited laboratory using controlled conditions.

That separation matters most for claims involving lead, PFAS, fluoride, arsenic, and other named contaminants. Read the exact row, initial concentration, reduction result, capacity, and notes in the current data rather than treating a headline contaminant count as the decision.

## Capacity and replacement math

The updated pitcher has an 80-ounce filtered-water compartment and an upper reservoir of about 64 ounces, or one-half gallon. The manufacturer rates the filter to 100 gallons and recommends changing it at 100 gallons or six months, whichever comes first.

The gallon limit usually arrives first in a busy household:

| Full upper-reservoir fills per day | Approximate gallons filtered per day | Time to 100 gallons | Practical implication |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | 0.5 | 200 days | Six-month date is likely to arrive first |
| 2 | 1.0 | 100 days | About three months, not six |
| 3 | 1.5 | 67 days | A little over two months |
| 4 | 2.0 | 50 days | Replacement becomes frequent |
| 5 | 2.5 | 40 days | A higher-capacity system deserves comparison |

This is planning math, not a guarantee. Clearly Filtered warns that local water conditions can affect speed and filter life. Count water used for coffee, cooking, pet bowls, and bottles rather than estimating only drinking glasses.

## What the pitcher is designed to treat

The manufacturer specifies municipally treated tap water. Its performance page explicitly says the filter is not designed for microbiologically unsafe water or water of unknown quality. That rules out using it as a stand-alone answer for a boil-water notice, untreated surface water, or an untested private well.

For municipal water, start with the annual consumer confidence report and any building-specific concern. Older plumbing can change the question between the utility main and the kitchen tap, so a named concern may justify appropriate sampling rather than buying from a generalized fear list.

The pitcher also does not soften a home. It will not stop scale across showers, appliances, and plumbing. A gravity pitcher serves drinking and cooking water at one point of use; whole-house hardness needs a correctly sized softening decision.

If the problem shows up beyond drinking water, use the [whole-house versus point-of-use decision](/vs/whole-house-water-filtration-vs-under-sink-drinking-water-filtration-which-one-fits-your-home/) first.

## Physical fit and daily use

Clearly Filtered lists the updated pitcher at 11.4 inches long, 5.25 inches wide, and 10.87 inches high, with an empty weight of 2.8 pounds. Measure the refrigerator shelf or door where it will actually live. A narrow body still needs clearance for the handle, lid, filling, and removal.

The 80-ounce lower capacity is enough for several glasses, but the 64-ounce upper reservoir means the fill cycle does not replenish the entire lower volume in one identical measure. In a multi-person household, filtered water can become a queue: somebody pours, somebody refills, and gravity needs time before the next large draw.

WaterFilterGuru’s updated 2026 assessment found the pitcher easy to set up and maintain and reported favorable filtration speed in its own protocol. That is useful independent context, not a promise for every source water or cartridge age. Gravity flow can change with water condition, trapped air, priming, and filter loading.

## The maintenance routine

The pitcher avoids plumbing but not maintenance:

1. Prime and install the element exactly as the current quick-start guide directs.
2. Use cold or room-temperature municipally treated tap water within the published range.
3. Keep a refill tally or dated gallon estimate rather than relying on memory.
4. Replace at 100 gallons or six months, whichever comes first, and sooner when guidance calls for it.
5. Hand-wash using the current care instructions and avoid abrasive pads that can scratch the body.
6. Investigate a change in flow, taste, odor, color, or source-water status rather than assuming the pitcher can solve it.

The no-install format is most valuable when the user can keep that loop simple. If the pitcher is constantly empty, a larger countertop or under-sink system may reduce refill friction. If replacement is regularly delayed, the theoretical performance table no longer describes a system used as intended.

## Clearly Filtered versus alternatives

| Alternative | Where it wins | Where Clearly Filtered wins |
|---|---|---|
| Basic carbon pitcher | Lower replacement burden for simple taste and odor | Broader published data when a named concern matches it |
| Zero-TDS-style pitcher | Better when dissolved-solids removal is the explicit goal | Does not frame zero TDS as the universal definition of good water |
| Faucet-mounted filter | Immediate water without carrying a pitcher | No faucet compatibility issue and renter-friendly |
| Under-sink carbon | Higher daily capacity and less refill work | No plumbing and portable at move-out |
| Under-sink reverse osmosis | Better for certain verified dissolved-contaminant goals and high use | No drain, tank, pump, power, or cabinet installation |

A simpler pitcher is sensible when chlorine taste or odor is the only issue and an appropriate NSF 42-certified model meets the need. Clearly Filtered earns its premium position only when its documented wider treatment scope is relevant to actual water evidence.

## Who should buy it

Choose it when all of these are true:

- The source is municipally treated tap water.
- A utility report or appropriate test identifies the concern.
- The current performance data addresses that concern at a useful capacity.
- The distinction between certification and added laboratory testing is acceptable.
- Half-gallon refills and the resulting replacement frequency fit the household.
- A portable no-install format matters more than high output.

That combination is common for renters and one- or two-person households. It is less convincing for a family filtering several gallons a day or a homeowner already prepared for an under-sink installation.

## Who should skip it

Skip the pitcher when a health authority has issued instructions, microbial safety is unknown, or a private well has not been appropriately tested. Treatment selected from a retailer list is not a substitute for source-specific guidance.

Skip it when scale, sediment, odor, or staining affects every fixture. That is a scope problem, and a kitchen pitcher cannot protect showers, a water heater, laundry, or plumbing.

Skip it when certification for a particular reduction claim is a firm requirement. Search the certifier’s current listing for the exact model and standard rather than extending the 42/372 certifications to the entire third-party table. The [five-gate NSF certification check](/guides/nsf-water-filter-certification-42-53-58-401-claims/) explains how to trace a standard number to the exact claim, system, replacement element, and current listing.

Finally, skip it when two or more daily reservoir fills sound tedious. At that rate, filter changes arrive around every 100 days or sooner, and the household may be happier with a properly selected plumbed system.

## Bottom line

The Clearly Filtered Water Pitcher is worth considering for broad, evidence-led filtration of municipally treated drinking water without installation. Its strongest feature is not the headline contaminant count; it is the accessible performance page that lets a careful buyer examine exact claims and limits.

The purchase works when you keep the evidence categories separate, confirm the target in your own water, and accept the refill and filter schedule. It fails when “NSF certified” is read as certification for every listed contaminant or when a point-of-use pitcher is asked to solve unsafe, unknown, or whole-house water.

### Is the Clearly Filtered pitcher NSF certified?

Yes, to NSF/ANSI 42 for chlorine taste and odor reduction and NSF/ANSI 372 for lead-content requirements. Its wider reduction table is additional third-party laboratory evidence, not certification to every listed standard or claim.

### How long does one filter last?

The rated capacity is 100 gallons, and replacement is recommended at 100 gallons or six months, whichever comes first. Two half-gallon fills each day reach 100 gallons in about 100 days.

### Does it remove lead?

The current performance table publishes a lead-reduction result from additional laboratory testing. Do not confuse NSF/ANSI 372 with lead-reduction certification: Standard 372 addresses lead content in product materials.

### Can it filter well water?

The manufacturer designs it for municipally treated tap water and says it is not for microbiologically unsafe or unknown water. Test a private well appropriately and follow health-authority guidance.

### Does it reduce TDS?

Do not use a TDS reading as a universal performance verdict. The pitcher is not a zero-TDS system, and total dissolved solids do not identify individual contaminants or prove safety.

### Is it worth the premium?

It can be when the broader published test data matches a verified concern and portability matters. A simpler certified carbon pitcher is better value when chlorine taste and odor are the only goal.
