## Start at the Main Cold-Water Line

This placement sends treated water to indoor fixtures while keeping the equipment accessible for cartridge changes and repairs.

A practical layout is:

1. Water meter or well pressure tank  
2. Existing main shutoff  
3. Inlet isolation valve  
4. Sediment filter, when sediment is present  
5. Carbon filter or another treatment stage  
6. Water softener, when hardness treatment is needed  
7. Outlet isolation valve  
8. Bypass line connecting the inlet and outlet sides  

Use full-port ball valves that match the main pipe size. A reduced-size valve can become a restriction even when the filter housings have larger ports.

Do not install a whole-house filter ahead of the water meter. Keep UV equipment after sediment filtration, not before it. UV treatment needs clear water to work through its chamber effectively.

Mount housings to solid framing, a wall, or an approved bracket. Water-filled housings are heavy, and unsupported pipe can strain fittings, particularly on PEX, CPVC, and older plastic plumbing.

Leave enough space below each housing to remove the full cartridge, plus room for a filter wrench, bucket, and wet cartridge.

## Choose Treatment for the Water Problem

More stages do not automatically mean better water treatment. Each additional housing, cartridge, valve, and fitting adds pressure drop and maintenance. Start with the water issue you are trying to solve.

| Water issue | Treatment approach | Important setup note |
|---|---|---|
| Sand, rust, silt, or visible sediment | Sediment filtration | A 20-micron filter catches larger grit; 5 microns captures finer sediment but clogs sooner. |
| Chlorine taste or odor | Activated carbon | Look for NSF/ANSI 42 claims for chlorine reduction. |
| Specific health-related contaminant claims | Certified treatment matched to the contaminant | NSF/ANSI 53 covers certain health-related reduction claims. |
| Chloramine-treated municipal water | Carbon media rated for chloramine reduction | Standard chlorine reduction does not automatically mean chloramine reduction. |
| Hard-water scale | Water softener | Carbon improves taste and reduces chlorine exposure but does not remove hardness minerals at the level needed to stop scale. |
| Iron, manganese, sulfur odor, or staining | Treatment based on laboratory analysis | These issues may require oxidation, specialized media, or aeration before final filtration. |
| Bacteria concerns on a private well | UV treatment after sediment filtration | UV needs clear water and controlled flow through the chamber. |

For private wells, annual testing for total coliform bacteria, nitrate, total dissolved solids, and pH creates a useful baseline. Add iron, manganese, arsenic, lead, and other local concerns when regional groundwater conditions, staining, odor, or taste point to a problem.

Municipal water reports explain the utility supply, but they cannot show what happens inside a home's plumbing. Lead exposure can be tied to service lines, solder, fixtures, and water sitting in pipes.

## Plan the Flow Rate Before Buying Equipment

A whole-house system must handle the water used when several fixtures run at once, not just the flow from one bathroom faucet.

For a modest one-bath home with limited simultaneous use, plan for at least 10 GPM. Homes where two showers, appliances, and hose bibs may operate at the same time should plan for 15 GPM or more.

Use these rules when laying out the system:

- Build around peak household demand rather than average daily water use.
- Keep the valve train the same size as the main pipe where possible.
- Use full-port valves rather than restrictive valves.
- Install pressure gauges before and after the filter train.
- Replace cartridges when pressure drop reaches 10 PSI from the clean baseline, unless the system instructions call for earlier service.

A larger housing can hold more filter media and usually allows longer intervals between cartridge changes than a smaller cartridge housing. That matters in homes with heavy sediment, where a fine cartridge can clog quickly.

Finer filtration is a trade-off. A 5-micron cartridge catches smaller particles than a coarse sediment screen, but it also collects material faster. For well water carrying a lot of grit or rust, a coarse prefilter or spin-down separator ahead of the finer cartridge can prevent frequent shutdowns.

Outdoor irrigation can also be separated before treatment. Lawn watering can consume carbon capacity and load sediment filters without improving water used indoors.

## Set Up the Valves and Bypass Loop

The bypass loop is part of the installation, not an optional extra. It lets water continue moving through the home while a cartridge is being changed, a housing is being repaired, or the filter train is being isolated.

The basic arrangement uses three control points:

- An inlet isolation valve before the filter train
- An outlet isolation valve after the filter train
- A bypass valve on a line that connects the inlet side to the outlet side

During normal operation, water flows through the filters while the bypass remains closed. During service, close the inlet and outlet valves and open the bypass.

Keep detachable fittings or unions near the equipment. They make future replacement easier than cutting pipe from a wall or rebuilding a long section of plumbing.

## Match the Layout to Your Water Source

### City water with chlorine taste or odor

Carbon filtration is the usual treatment route for chlorine taste and odor. Add a sediment stage only when sediment is visible or documented. Clear municipal water does not always need a sediment cartridge, and an unnecessary stage adds another cartridge to replace.

### City water with hard-water scale

Use a softener when hardness is causing scale. Carbon can go ahead of the softener when chlorine reduction is needed to protect softener resin and improve taste.

If the concern is limited to a dissolved contaminant in drinking water, a point-of-use system at the kitchen tap may be more appropriate than treating every gallon used for bathing, laundry, and toilets.

### Private well with grit or rust

Start with sediment management. Heavy particulate load can overwhelm a fine cartridge, so a spin-down separator or coarse prefilter may belong before a finer sediment stage.

### Private well with odor, staining, iron, or manganese

Use laboratory results to determine the treatment order. Iron, manganese, pH, and hydrogen sulfide can interact, and carbon alone is rarely a complete answer for persistent staining or rotten-egg odor.

### Older home with a drinking-water concern

A whole-house sediment and carbon system can improve general household water, but it is not automatically the answer to every drinking-water concern. A point-of-use system at the kitchen tap directs treatment to water used for drinking and cooking.

## Leave Room for Service

The easiest installation to maintain is one that accounts for spills, wet housings, and cartridge removal from the start.

Place the system near a floor drain, utility sink, or washable surface when possible. In a finished basement or tight utility closet, keep a shallow drain pan, bucket, towels, and filter wrench nearby.

A useful service setup includes:

- Spare cartridges stored clean and dry
- Replacement O-rings
- Food-grade silicone lubricant when directed for the housing O-ring
- A filter wrench
- Pressure gauges before and after the system
- A record of installation dates, cartridge types, and clean pressure readings
- A drain pan beneath housings where a leak could damage flooring

Change cartridges based on the service schedule, rated gallon capacity, and pressure drop. Do not wait until water tastes bad. A sediment cartridge can restrict flow long before it changes taste or odor.

To change a cartridge:

1. Close the inlet and outlet isolation valves.  
2. Open the bypass valve.  
3. Relieve pressure from the filter housing.  
4. Place a bucket beneath the sump.  
5. Remove the housing and cartridge.  
6. Clean the housing according to its instructions.  
7. Inspect the O-ring for flattening or damage.  
8. Lubricate the O-ring with food-grade silicone lubricant if directed.  
9. Tighten the housing evenly.  
10. Return the valves to the normal filtration position and watch for leaks.  

## Installation Limits That Matter

Measure the pipe, wall space, and service area before choosing housings or treatment equipment.

Pay attention to the following:

- **Pipe diameter:** Do not route a 1-inch main through a restrictive 3/4-inch valve train without accounting for possible flow loss.
- **Housing ports:** Larger ports can help, but internal filter design and cartridge media still affect actual flow.
- **Water pressure:** Many residential plumbing systems operate below 80 PSI. Local plumbing code sets the governing limit, and a pressure-reducing valve is needed where supply pressure exceeds the allowed range.
- **Water temperature:** Whole-house housings on the cold-water main are not automatically suitable for hot-water service.
- **Freeze exposure:** Filters in garages, crawlspaces, and exterior utility areas need freeze protection. A frozen housing can crack and flood the area when pressure returns.
- **Drain access:** Softeners, backwashing filters, and some iron-treatment systems need a proper drain connection and air-gap arrangement.

## Understand Certification Claims

Certification standards help narrow the choices, but a certification mark applies only to the stated claim.

- **NSF/ANSI 42:** Aesthetic effects, including chlorine taste and odor  
- **NSF/ANSI 53:** Reduction claims for specific health-related contaminants  
- **NSF/ANSI 58:** Reverse osmosis systems  
- **NSF/ANSI 55:** UV microbiological treatment systems  
- **NSF/ANSI 44:** Residential water softeners  

Read the performance data sheet for the specific substances reduced, rated flow rate, and cartridge change interval. Carbon media alone is not proof of lead reduction, for example; lead reduction requires a documented claim for that contaminant.

## When a Whole-House Cartridge Filter Is Not the Right Tool

A basic whole-house cartridge setup is not a cure-all. Stop before installing one if the problem points to a source issue or a treatment method that needs to be designed around water results.

Persistent sewage-like odor, bacterial contamination, very high nitrate, severe iron staining, and recurring turbidity call for testing and targeted treatment rather than a standard sediment-and-carbon setup.

A whole-house system can also be unnecessary when the concern is limited to drinking water. Treating every gallon used for showers, toilets, laundry, and cleaning makes little sense when only water consumed at the kitchen sink needs attention.

Renters should avoid permanent main-line modifications without written approval. Countertop and under-sink filters create less plumbing disruption and are easier to remove later.

## Common Setup Mistakes

Do not use a fine sediment cartridge as the only answer to rusty or sandy well water. It can clog quickly and turn routine maintenance into frequent water shutoffs.

Do not add carbon treatment simply because it is common in multi-stage systems. Carbon is useful for chlorine, chloramine, taste, odor, and documented contaminant claims. It does not replace a softener or treatment for dissolved metals.

Do not skip the bypass loop. Every filter housing eventually needs service, and the bypass prevents a cartridge change from shutting off water to the entire house.

Do not combine thread sealant, fittings, or pipe transitions that conflict with the plumbing already in place. Local plumbing code and the equipment installation instructions govern the connection method.

## Quick Installation Checklist

- Identify whether the home uses municipal water or a private well.
- Review the water report or arrange testing for the concerns present.
- Trace the main cold-water line after the meter or pressure tank.
- Measure the main pipe diameter and available wall space.
- Record static water pressure.
- Identify peak demand from showers, appliances, and outdoor faucets.
- Decide whether irrigation should bypass treatment.
- Install inlet and outlet isolation valves with a bypass loop.
- Leave clearance below housings for cartridge removal and a bucket.
- Include pressure gauges before and after the treatment train.
- Confirm drain requirements for softeners, backwashing equipment, or specialty treatment.
- Store replacement cartridges and O-rings clean, dry, and labeled.

## Bottom Line

Install whole-house filtration after the main shutoff and before indoor water branches. Use inlet and outlet isolation valves, a bypass loop, pressure gauges, and enough open space to service the housings without making a mess.

Build the treatment train around the water problem. Sediment and carbon filtration can address common household concerns, while hardness, iron, sulfur odor, bacteria, nitrate, and lead require treatment selected for those specific issues.

## FAQ

### Should a whole-house filter go before or after the water softener?

Install sediment filtration ahead of both carbon and softening equipment when particulate matter could clog media or damage equipment. Install carbon before a softener when chlorine or chloramine reduction is needed to protect softener resin. Well-water treatment order depends on iron, manganese, pH, and other water results.

### Do I need a bypass valve for a whole-house water filter?

Yes. A bypass keeps water flowing during cartridge replacement, leak repair, and troubleshooting. It also lets you isolate the filter system when pressure drops suddenly or a housing needs service.

### What micron sediment filter should I use for a whole house?

Use 20 microns for larger grit and visible sediment. Move to 5 microns when finer particles remain. Water with heavy sediment often benefits from a coarse first stage ahead of a 5-micron cartridge.

### Will a whole-house filter reduce water pressure?

Yes. Every filter stage creates some pressure drop. Full-port valves, properly sized housings, and pressure gauges help manage it. Replace cartridges when downstream pressure falls about 10 PSI below the clean baseline or when the system instructions call for earlier service.

### Does a whole-house carbon filter remove lead?

Only a filter with a documented lead-reduction claim addresses lead. Carbon media by itself is not evidence of lead reduction. Water testing that reflects the home's plumbing exposure helps determine whether targeted treatment is needed.
