A bucket test measures **delivery at one outlet under recorded conditions**. It does not certify whole-house capacity, diagnose the filter by itself or measure contaminant reduction. The most useful result is a repeatable comparison at the same outlet before and after a relevant change.

## Choose a manageable volume and known water path

Use a clean container with a reliable volume mark, a timer and a cold-water outlet you know is downstream of the equipment being assessed. A five-US-gallon fill is not mandatory; use a smaller accurately measured volume if that is easier to handle safely. Prevent splashing and avoid lifting an unnecessarily heavy container.

Record the outlet, any hose or aerator, valve position and other water use. A flow-restricted faucet is valid for measuring that faucet's delivery; it is a poor basis for declaring the main filter undersized. Do not assume an outdoor tap is filtered, or remove a fixture's flow control to obtain a more impressive number.

## Calculate from total volume and total time

For US gallons:

**GPM = collected US gallons × 60 ÷ elapsed seconds.**

For litres, the same formula gives litres per minute. Keep US and Imperial gallons separate.

Here is a worked example using three equal two-US-gallon fills:

| Fill | Volume | Time |
|---|---:|---:|
| First | 2 gal | 24 s |
| Second | 2 gal | 26 s |
| Third | 2 gal | 25 s |
| Total | 6 gal | 75 s |

The combined result is **6 × 60 ÷ 75 = 4.8 GPM**. Using total volume and total time also works if collected volumes differ, provided each volume is measured accurately. Do not average unequal-duration flow readings as though each represented the same amount of water.

## Make the comparison interpretable

Start with a repeatable ordinary condition. Record other fixtures that are running and the stage of a well-pump cycle if known. Repeat measurements when timing varies enough to change the decision; do not hide a large spread inside one average.

Compare with the same outlet, accessories and household-use condition after scheduled service. A hypothetical change from 4.8 to 6.0 GPM is a 25% increase in measured delivery, but it still does not prove that the filter alone caused the change if incoming pressure or other demand also changed.

There is no universal pass line of 3, 5 or 8 GPM for every outlet. Compare the result with the fixture's intended operation, the system's documented duty and an earlier like-for-like record. A low-flow showerhead can be working as designed below those arbitrary figures.

## Add pressure evidence when available

If suitable gauges are installed, note inlet and outlet pressure during the same draw. The [pressure-loss guide](/guides/how-to-reduce-pressure-drop-in-whole-house-filtration-systems/) explains how those readings help distinguish supply loss from restriction within the measured section.

Do not bypass treatment just to improve the number. Where a qualified service procedure permits a controlled bypass comparison, account for untreated water and protect users and downstream equipment. Without that authorization and control, compare normal operation before and after service instead.

## Use a different test for water quality

Clear water and strong flow do not demonstrate contaminant reduction. Use the [filter-performance guide](/guides/how-to-test-your-whole-house-water-filter-s-performance-at-home-beginner-steps/) for claim-matched testing and the [laboratory-panel guide](/guides/how-to-choose-a-well-water-test-panel-for-your-home/) for source or health-related concerns.

The formula and worked example are direct volume/time arithmetic. No household flow measurement was performed for this article, and the result is not a well-yield test or plumbing design.
