For a new well-water homeowner, the first useful purchase is often the right laboratory assessment, not another filter. Start by recovering the well and treatment history, then verify what reaches the taps. Existing equipment is evidence of a previous decision, not proof that today's water is suitable.

## Assemble a handover folder

Ask the seller or installer for the well log, pump and pressure-system details, recent laboratory reports, treatment manuals, service dates and replacement-part codes. Note whether a sample was taken before or after treatment. Photograph model labels and trace which branches bypass treatment without operating unfamiliar valves.

Mark missing records as unknown. A clear-looking tank, an estate-agent description or a previous owner's “never had a problem” does not fill the gap.

## Four gates before a treatment purchase

| Gate | Evidence to obtain | Decision it unlocks |
|---|---|---|
| Water assessment | Appropriate laboratory panel and local risk advice | Which problems actually need attention |
| Existing treatment | Exact models, operating condition and service history | What can be retained, serviced or needs reassessment |
| Supply and installation | Available flow/pressure, space, drains and power | Which supported designs are feasible |
| Verification plan | Required post-work sample and service instructions | How to establish the intended treatment is working |

[EPA](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) advises annual testing for total coliform, nitrate, TDS and pH, with additional testing driven by local risks and changes. Use a suitable certified laboratory and local health advice to choose the complete panel. Recent flooding, repair or a change in appearance, taste or odor can require prompt investigation instead of waiting for the annual date.

## Do not force every result into “whole-house first”

The right treatment location depends on the contaminant, exposure pathways and permitted treatment method. Detecting nitrate does not by itself prove that an entire-house system is the only option. Equally, a kitchen device cannot be assumed to protect bathing, other taps or plumbing. A qualified treatment plan should name both the contaminant and the water uses it covers.

If microbiological contamination or another health concern is suspected, follow local advice on water use and source correction. A sediment cartridge is not a disinfection plan. Avoid buying on “purifies everything” language.

## Make the installer explain the handover

Before accepting a proposed system, request a labeled flow diagram, exact treatment claims, operating limits, replacement schedule and a record of commissioning. Identify who handles alarms, leaks, repeat testing and consumables. Keep the untreated-water route clear on the diagram so later sampling is meaningful.
