A water-test result is a measurement from a particular sample and method. It is not a whole-home safety certificate. Before buying treatment, check **what was measured, where the sample came from, the units and the method's reporting limit**.

## Read the report in this order

| Field | Why it can change your interpretation |
|---|---|
| Sample location | Raw well water, treated tap and refrigerator water can answer different questions |
| Collection instructions | Stagnation, flushing and bottle handling depend on the analyte and purpose |
| Analyte and form | A total measurement and a dissolved measurement need not mean the same thing |
| Units | mg/L and µg/L differ by a factor of 1,000 |
| Result and reporting limit | “Not detected” means below the method's stated limit, not necessarily absent |
| Qualifiers | Holding-time, preservation or quality-control issues may limit use of the number |
| Relevant benchmark | Use the applicable jurisdiction and health authority's interpretation |

For example, **0.010 mg/L equals 10 µg/L**. This is a unit conversion, not a statement that the concentration is acceptable for a particular substance. Compare like units before interpreting a result.

## Decide what kind of evidence you have

A home strip may be useful for a screening question such as approximate hardness. Its color resolution, expiry and handling limit precision. A TDS meter estimates dissolved-solids content through conductivity; it does not identify lead, nitrate or microbes individually.

For health-related decisions, use a laboratory with the appropriate certification or accreditation for the analyte and drinking-water method. A large number of listed parameters is less useful than the correct method for the question. Ask the laboratory about a result it flags or qualifies.

## Act on a concerning result without waiting for a shopping decision

Contact the utility or local health authority promptly when contamination is suspected or a health-related result is concerning. Follow its advice on water use and confirmatory sampling. Do not keep using questionable water solely because a repeat sample has not arrived, and do not assume boiling solves chemical contamination.

Do not use an arbitrary “close enough” margin around a health benchmark. Nor should you repeat samples until one looks reassuring and discard the others. Keep the original result, collection details and any follow-up report together.

## Keep sampling purpose consistent

There is no universal instruction to flush every tap or always take a first-draw sample. Lead investigations, microbiological samples and source-water checks may require different procedures. Get the bottles and written instructions from the laboratory before collecting water. Do not rinse a supplied bottle unless instructed.

After treatment, verify the particular treatment objective with a suitable method. Clear water, improved taste or lower TDS cannot confirm unrelated reductions.
