A softener and a whole-house reverse-osmosis system answer different questions. **Measure hardness separately from any named contaminant, then decide which outlets need treatment.** A high TDS reading alone does not establish either a health problem or a need for whole-house RO.

## Use two columns in the treatment brief

| Question | Hardness column | Other water-quality column |
|---|---|---|
| Measurement | Hardness in grains per gallon or mg/L as calcium carbonate | Each named constituent, its units and laboratory result |
| Intended result | Reduce calcium and magnesium hardness | Meet the specific treatment objective for that constituent |
| Candidate process | Appropriate ion-exchange softening | A process with evidence for the particular reduction claim |
| Coverage | Which appliances and household branches need softened water? | Which exposure routes and outlets require protection? |
| Verification | Appropriate before-and-after hardness measurement | Suitable constituent-specific testing and commissioning |

Do not substitute a conductivity-based TDS meter for either column. Softening exchanges ions; it does not necessarily produce a large TDS reduction. Conversely, a lower TDS result after RO does not prove a particular contaminant is below its applicable limit.

## A home can have two separate requirements

Suppose testing confirms hardness and a separate drinking-water concern. Ask for a hardness-treatment proposal and a constituent-specific proposal. One possible design is whole-house softening plus suitable point-of-use drinking-water treatment. Another source may require a broader engineered system. The laboratory evidence, local guidance and exposure route decide between them.

An RO membrane can reject hardness minerals, but that does not eliminate pretreatment planning. Hardness can contribute to membrane scaling. A softener may therefore sit upstream of RO rather than compete with it. Ask the designer to explain feed limits, recovery, pretreatment, storage, repressurization and post-treatment for the exact proposed system.

## Request two quotes with the same evidence

Give each installer the same dated water report, required treated outlets, peak demand and site constraints. Request:

1. A hardness-focused option with its service flow, capacity at the proposed salt setting, regeneration water and discharge requirements.
2. If separately justified, an option addressing the other confirmed concern, with the exact reduction evidence and required coverage.
3. An explanation of which original problem remains untreated by each quote.
4. A commissioning and maintenance plan, including who verifies performance after installation.

Comparing only purchase prices hides different scopes. A softener does not become a nitrate or microbial treatment because its water feels different. An RO package does not cover every contaminant merely because it includes a membrane.

## When to narrow the purchase

If hardness is the established objective, start with a properly sized softener proposal. If the requirement is confined to suitable drinking-water outlets, evaluate appropriate point-of-use treatment before committing to whole-house RO. For broader concerns, obtain a design tied to the actual laboratory results.

This comparison is a treatment-planning aid, not a diagnosis or a hands-on product test. The [USGS hardness explanation](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/hardness-water) defines the measurement; [NSF's standards guide](https://www.nsf.org/consumer-resources/articles/standards-water-treatment-systems) explains why softener and RO certification scopes differ. Neither substitutes for checking the exact model and claim.
